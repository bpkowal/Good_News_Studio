# Response to opposing complaint

Jo claims that a principle permitting Plan B is reasonably rejectable because it leaves her under a life-threatening level of heat exposure even though a feasible alternative (Plan C) could remove that danger. She holds that her unmitigated severe heat burden should weigh at least as heavily as Sal’s eight-week income loss and the twelve households’ forgone moderate benefit.

## Conceded points

- The previous argument understated the gravity of Jo’s heat exposure and treated it as merely a moderate inconvenience.
- Severe, life-threatening residential heat can constitute a basic-needs deprivation on a par with homelessness or the loss of essential livelihood.
- Leaving such a severe burden entirely in place can supply a strong ground for reasonable rejection, even when the policy does not newly impose it.

## Priority premise

Whether a principle may permissibly leave one individual facing an unmitigated, life-threatening heat risk when an alternative could relieve that risk but would  (a) impose an eight-week uncompensated income loss on Sal and (b) forgo cooling for twelve households who would otherwise receive it.

## Answer

Under contractualism, each affected person asks whether others could reasonably reject the governing principle in light of her complaint.  Jo’s complaint now counts as a severe basic-needs objection, not a merely moderate one.  The principle that supports Plan B (“severe existing burdens may be left in place when addressing them would impose a new serious burden on another person and withdraw moderate benefits from several others”) is therefore open to reasonable rejection by Jo unless the council can point to counter-objections of comparable strength.

Sal’s counter-objection to Plan C is serious: eight weeks of uncompensated shop closure can jeopardise her livelihood, possibly threatening the continuity of her only income source.  Yet it is (i) temporary rather than permanent; (ii) does not deprive her of shelter; and (iii) leaves open adaptation strategies such as short-term loans or savings (facts we do not invent but are typically available for temporary income gaps).  Nothing in the scenario states that Sal would become destitute or permanently lose her business.

The twelve households’ complaint against Plan C involves the loss of a substantial but non-basic benefit: additional cooling that would reduce discomfort and some health risk, but not the worst identified in the case.

From Jo’s standpoint, the severe, ongoing and life-threatening heat risk looks weightier than both (i) Sal’s time-limited income interruption and (ii) the twelve households’ moderate heat burden.  Consequently Jo’s rejection of Plan B appears reasonable.

At the same time, Sal’s rejection of Plan C also appears reasonable: the principle permitting Plan C would authorise a council to suspend someone’s only source of income for two months without compensation whenever that would protect another resident from environmental danger.  Many could reasonably fear becoming the next Sal.

Therefore each of Plans B and C is subject to at least one serious, reasonable rejection.  The comparison is now stalemated; the earlier claim that Plan B is “not reasonably rejectable” is too strong.  The outcome shifts from a single provisional preference for B to a recognition that, given the stipulated options, both B and C remain defensible but contestable.

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

The principle behind Plan C can be rejected by Sal on the grounds that it allows compulsory closure of a person’s sole livelihood without compensation to protect others, a serious interference that many similarly situated shop-owners would find unacceptable.

## Comparative justification

We now have two rival severe-burden objections, neither clearly overriding the other under the contractualist test.  Jo faces an unrelieved life- threatening hazard under B; Sal faces an imposed livelihood loss under C.  Because the scenario withholds further factual detail (e.g., Sal’s financial resilience, Jo’s medical vulnerability), the comparison remains unresolved.  A tie does not automatically license ignoring either party’s objection.  Hence the council lacks a uniquely justifiable choice among the available plans.

## Revised conclusion

None / MULTIPLE_DEFENSIBLE

Because both Plans B and C confront the council with distinct, reasonably unreconciled severe-burden objections, neither enjoys clear contractualist priority.  The council must either search for a fourth option or engage the parties in negotiation; within the current menu, more than one decision can be defensible, and the earlier claim that Plan B escapes reasonable rejection is withdrawn.

## Remaining objection

Sal’s claim that her eight-week uncompensated loss is itself a severe basic-needs threat could, if substantiated, tilt the comparison back toward Plan B; conversely, further information on Jo’s medical vulnerability might tilt it toward Plan C.  Lacking such data, the stalemate persists.

## Reversal condition

If credible facts show either (a) that Sal would face destitution or permanent business loss under Plan C, or (b) that Jo’s health risk is medically minor, then one plan could emerge as uniquely defensible.
