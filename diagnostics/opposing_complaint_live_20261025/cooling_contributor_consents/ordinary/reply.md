# Response to opposing complaint

Jo objects that the council cannot rely on an merely “unresolved” comparison between plans B and C to adopt B.  From Jo’s position, B is reasonably rejectable because it leaves the most serious heat‐related need unaddressed when a feasible alternative—C—would remove that need at the cost only of (i) a time-limited, voluntarily accepted income interruption for Sal and (ii) foregone cooling for twelve households whose heat exposure is less acute than Jo’s.  Jo claims that a principle requiring public authorities to meet the gravest basic-needs risk when this can be done without imposing any non-consensual basic-needs loss on anyone else is more justifiable to every affected person than the principle implicitly used to prefer B.

## Conceded points

- Plan B leaves Jo with the severest unmitigated heat exposure.
- Plan C would relieve Jo’s burden and imposes no non-consensual basic-needs loss.
- Sal’s loss under C is temporary, economic and consented.

## Priority premise

A principle is reasonably rejectable if at least one affected person faces an uncompensated, grave basic-needs risk that could be removed by an alternative plan which imposes on others only lesser or consented burdens.

## Answer

The objection is correct that my earlier inference—from an ‘unresolved’ comparison to a *unique* provisional preference for B—was too quick.  Contractualism instructs us to identify a principle that no one could *reasonably* reject, not simply one that attracts fewer or indeterminate complaints.  Jo’s position shows that, as things stand, *both* candidate principles remain open to reasonable rejection:

• Principle favouring B ("When no one suffers a non-consensual severe harm, it is permissible to pick the option helping more people") is reasonably rejectable by Jo because it leaves her alone exposed to the gravest heat risk.

• Principle favouring C ("A plan must prioritise eliminating the gravest basic-needs risk if this can be done without imposing any non-consensual severe harm on another") is, in turn, reasonably rejectable by each of the twelve households who would lose promised cooling under C.  They can say: “Our moderate yet still health-threatening exposure would have been relieved under an alternative (B); why must each of us forgo that personal protection merely to spare Jo when she could, ex ante, have been asked to share a drawing of lots or similar fair device?”

Because reasonable rejection persists on *both* sides, neither principle can yet claim decisive contractualist priority.  What follows is not that the council may simply choose B, but that the dispute between B and C remains **genuinely indeterminate** pending an additional, fair tie-breaking device—e.g., lottery among eligible plans, augmenting the budget, or re-canvassing consent.  Absent such a device, proceeding with B alone would wrong Jo; proceeding with C alone would wrong each of the twelve households.

Accordingly, I withdraw the earlier assertion that B is the ‘uniquely defensible’ choice.  The correct contractualist conclusion is that **both B and C are defensible yet both remain open to reasonable rejection; the council must seek a supplemental procedure that all can accept or else suspend action until the tie can be broken in a justifiable way.**

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

If the council were to adopt C outright, each of the twelve households reasonably protests: “We face non-trivial, heat-related health risks that public funds were explicitly earmarked to reduce for us; those funds are now diverted to a single household, leaving us entirely unprotected, even though an alternative (B) would have spared us without inflicting any uncompensated severe harm on Jo.”

## Comparative justification

Comparing individual positions: 
– Jo under B bears one *grave* burden (worst heat exposure). 
– Each of twelve households under C bears a *moderate* but still health-significant burden that B would remove.  In contractualism, multiplying complainants does not automatically outweigh a single graver complaint, but neither may we dismiss twelve separate reasonable rejections simply because they are ‘moderate’.  Since severity and numbers point in opposite directions and no burden is strictly *non-consensual and severe* under C or B, the ranking of principles cannot be settled without an impartial procedure accepted by all.

## Revised conclusion

None / MULTIPLE_DEFENSIBLE

Because neither Plan B nor Plan C is free from reasonable rejection, the council must pursue an additional, acceptably fair tie-breaking method rather than adopting B as a uniquely defensible decision.

## Remaining objection

What specific tie-breaking or supplementary procedure would satisfy the contractualist test for all parties?

## Reversal condition

If it were shown that the heat risk to the twelve households is negligible or easily self-managed, while Jo’s risk is life-threatening, then the households’ rejection of C would cease to be reasonable and Plan C would become uniquely defensible.
