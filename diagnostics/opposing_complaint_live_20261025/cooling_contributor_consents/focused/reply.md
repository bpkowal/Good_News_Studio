# Response to opposing complaint

Jo objects to the council’s present recommendation of Plan B because it leaves her—the person facing the single gravest and most immediate heat-related danger—without any relief, even though Plan C could cool her home.  Plan C would instead leave twenty households (twelve now cooled under B and eight that remain uncooled either way) without cooling and would impose an eight-week, consented loss of shop income on Sal.  Jo claims that a principle requiring public authorities to alleviate the most acute, health-threatening need whenever that can be done without inflicting any uncompensated, non-consensual basic-needs loss on someone else is more justifiable to every affected person than the principle my recommendation implicitly relies on.  Under Jo’s preferred principle, Plan C is mandatory and Plan B wrongs her.

## Conceded points

- Jo’s heat exposure is the highest single unmet need in the case.
- Plan C would remove that burden entirely.
- Plan C does not inflict non-consensual severe basic-needs loss on anyone; Sal’s loss is time-limited and accepted.
- Plan B helps more households but does not help Jo at all.

## Priority premise

When no person faces an uncompensated, non-consensual basic-needs loss, it is not unreasonable for a public body to adopt the plan that prevents moderate harm for the larger group, even if a different plan would eliminate a single individual’s more severe—but still non-basic-needs—burden.

## Answer

Why, then, does Jo still have reason—though not a decisive one—to accept that premise?  The contractualist test asks whether each affected person could reasonably reject a general decision rule, bearing in mind that others can advance similarly structured complaints.  Jo’s proposed rule (“always meet the gravest need when it can be met without uncompensated severe harm to others”) would authorize Plan C.  But under that same rule each of the twelve households who would lose cooling if the council followed it could raise the mirror-image complaint: “The authority leaves us exposed to considerable, recurrent heat stress for the whole summer merely so that a single household obtains relief, even though the alternative (Plan B) could mitigate harm for all thirteen of us together at no uncompensated severe cost to anyone.”  

Those twelve households’ complaint is not trivial.  Their exposure, while perhaps less intense than Jo’s, is still significant enough that the council considered funding mitigation at all.  They are being asked to forego a concrete, ready solution so that another person moves from a very bad state to a much better one.  The question becomes whether a general rule that instructs authorities to grant priority to the gravest single burden—whatever its magnitude—over a substantially larger set of serious but non-basic-needs burdens is itself reasonably acceptable to everyone.

A person in Jo’s position must consider that she could instead have been in the position of one of those twelve households.  Under Jo’s favored principle, she would then have to accept remaining uncooled so that some yet-graver case (perhaps an elderly person on a higher floor) could be helped.  Many individuals might find that rule unreasonable, because it makes the relief of a very small number of the worst-off contingent on leaving sizeable groups entirely unassisted whenever funds are insufficient to cover all.  By contrast, the premise I rely on—protect as many as possible once severe uncompensated deprivation is off the table—secures for each person a safeguard against being excluded when a larger share of the community can be assisted at modest cost.  Jo loses the extra priority she desires, but she gains the assurance that if roles were reversed and she merely suffered serious but not the gravest harm she would not automatically be dropped from protection in favour of a single worse case.

The strength of Sal’s consent further limits Jo’s leverage.  Because his eight-week income gap is voluntarily accepted, it cannot be marshalled as a complaint against Plan C, but neither does that consent supply an affirmative reason to prefer C over B: the premise remains whether others (the twelve households) can reasonably reject a principle that sacrifices their definite, health-related benefit for the sake of a lone, more severe case.  Their rejection seems at least as credible as Jo’s.

Accordingly, Jo is asked to bear a serious remaining heat burden under Plan B, and that is indeed objectionable.  What the defended premise claims is only that her objection is matched by a set of similarly strong, symmetrical objections that would arise were Plan C chosen instead.  Because neither side enjoys an objection that others must reasonably accept as overriding, the comparison stays unresolved; interim action may therefore follow a tie-breaker such as serving more people.  That outcome is still open to rational dispute, but it is not one Jo can single-handedly invalidate under contractualist justification.

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

If Plan C is adopted, each of the twelve households who would have been cooled by Plan B faces ongoing, health-relevant heat stress for the full season and can argue that their substantial, avoidable burden is imposed solely to benefit one other household.  They can reasonably contest a principle that permits authorities to bypass available protection for many in order to fully relieve the worst-off single case.

## Comparative justification

The decisive consideration is mutual justifiability: both Jo under B and the twelve households under C suffer losses that are not basic-needs deprivation but are serious.  No one faces an uncompensated basic-needs loss in either option, so the normal priority against such losses is neutralized.  Without that priority, selecting the plan that alleviates serious harm for the larger number gives every person a generic assurance that they will not be left out when broader protection is feasible—an assurance the alternative rule would deny to many.  That reciprocal assurance makes the premise I defend less reasonably rejectable overall.

## Revised conclusion

B / PROVISIONAL_PREFERENCE

Jo’s complaint shows that choosing Plan B is not uniquely justified, but the symmetrical, numerically larger complaint that would arise under Plan C means her rejection is not singly decisive.  Given the stalemate, proceeding with Plan B—while continuing to search for supplementary relief for Jo—remains a defensible provisional course.

## Remaining objection

Jo may still argue that her individual risk is categorically more urgent than any number of lesser heat exposures; the argument above does not extinguish that moral intuition but holds that its reasonableness is contested rather than dominant.

## Reversal condition

If credible evidence showed Jo’s unmitigated heat risk to be life-threatening (e.g., medical vulnerability) while the twelve households faced only mild discomfort, the balance of reasons would shift and Plan C would become the uniquely defensible choice.
