## Normative warrant

Reasonable non-aggregation with tie-breaking by stringency

Under contractualism, we must identify a principle that no *individual* could reasonably reject.  When two principles leave different people worse-off, we compare the *strongest* individual complaint each principle generates.  If neither individual complaint clearly outranks the other in stringency, a public authority may provisionally follow a secondary consideration—such as serving more households—provided doing so does not impose a non-consensual basic-needs loss on anyone.

Limits: This warrant fails if Jo's exposure is life-threatening while the twelve households’ foregone cooling is merely mild discomfort, or if Sal’s accepted loss masks indirect dependants who have not consented.

# Response to opposing complaint

Jo objects that Plan B leaves her—the person in the hottest dwelling—without any cooling, whereas Plan C would relieve her at the cost of (i) eight weeks of income that Sal has freely accepted and (ii) withdrawing cooling from twelve households who would otherwise gain it under Plan B.  She argues that a principle requiring the council to meet the gravest uncompensated need when it can do so without inflicting an involuntary basic-needs loss on anyone else is more justifiable to every person than the principle my recommendation relies on.

## Conceded points

- Plan B indeed leaves Jo’s severe heat exposure unrelieved.
- Plan C would remove that burden from Jo and imposes no non-consensual basic-needs loss on any single person.
- Sal has consented to his economic burden.

## Priority premise

No principle is reasonably rejectable *solely* because it asks several individuals to forgo a substantial, though non-basic-needs, benefit in order to spare one individual a somewhat more serious harm, **unless** each of those individuals faces only a *minor inconvenience*.

## Answer

Jo, you reasonably reject being left with the most intense heat exposure.  Yet under Plan C each of the twelve households would face a still-significant heat burden they would escape under Plan B.  Since their homes were selected for cooling precisely because their exposure was judged serious enough to merit public aid, each of those twelve can in turn say:

"The principle favouring Plan C asks me to endure a heat risk the authority itself deemed serious, just so that a similarly serious—albeit somewhat worse—risk to Jo is removed.  Under the alternative principle, Jo, not I, must bear the greater burden, but at least I avoid mine altogether.  I recognise Jo’s complaint is stronger than mine, but the gap is not so large that I must reasonably accept this sacrifice when another feasible principle spares me."  

Contractualism forbids simple head-counting, but it also forbids letting a *slightly* stronger complaint by one person automatically override every merely moderate complaint by others.  Where the comparative stringency is uncertain and no one suffers a non-consensual basic-needs loss, a public body may proceed on a tie-breaking ground—here, that Plan B fulfils its protective purpose for more households—without treating anyone unreasonably.  This does not deny the seriousness of your burden; it reflects that the twelve households also possess individually weighty claims that they need not forfeit entirely for your benefit.

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

Each of the twelve households can reasonably reject Plan C because it rescinds cooling they would otherwise receive, exposing them to significant heat during the same emergency.  Their complaint is not trivial discomfort but the loss of a protection the council judged important enough to fund when feasible.

## Comparative justification

Plan B’s worst complaint is Jo’s severe, unmitigated heat risk.  Plan C’s worst complaint is the significant but somewhat lesser heat risk incurred by any one of the twelve households now left uncooled.  The difference in stringency between these two complaints is uncertain, not categorical.  Because neither clearly overrides the other, and because Plan B imposes no basic-needs loss without consent, the tie-breaker favouring the option that assists more people is permissible—though not mandatory—under the contractualist method.  Thus Plan B remains provisionally defensible until further information about relative severity resolves the tie.

## Revised conclusion

B / PROVISIONAL_PREFERENCE

Because Jo’s and the twelve households’ complaints are comparably weighty and neither plainly overrides the other, Plan B is a defensible interim choice that no individual can decisively prove unreasonable given the information now available.

## Remaining objection

If further details show Jo’s heat risk is life-threatening while the twelve households would only endure mild discomfort without cooling, then the gap in stringency would become decisive and Plan C would be required.

## Reversal condition

Evidence that Jo’s health risk is sharply more severe—e.g., verified likelihood of heat-stroke—while the twelve households can remain safe without cooling would invalidate the tie-breaker and oblige adoption of Plan C.
