## Normative warrant

Qualified Promise‐Override

A promise made without knowledge of a third person who would otherwise face much greater loss may be overridden when (i) keeping it would impose on that third person a substantially higher risk of death and (ii) the promisor accepts a residual duty of apology and reparation toward the promisee. Each person, ex ante, has reason to accept a rule that spares them from being sacrificed to an uninformed promise to someone else when their own risk would be markedly greater.

Limits: Does not license breaking promises for small advantages; the survival‐gap must be significant and no alternative that respects both lives must be available.

# Response to opposing complaint

Sasha objects that giving the blanket to Rowan (Action A) inflicts two special burdens on her: (1) a 100 % chance of death compared with only 40 % under Action B, and (2) the betrayal of a specific assurance that she, uniquely, had been given. The feasible alternative—keeping the promise (Action B)—reduces her risk of death and avoids betrayal. She argues that under contractualism the moral status created by the promise gives her a defeasible right that a mere 20-percentage-point survival gap for Rowan cannot reasonably override.

## Conceded points

- Ellis did make a clear, voluntary promise to Sasha.
- Sasha would die with certainty under Action A and faces only a 40 % risk under Action B.
- Betrayal of a life-saving promise is a serious relational harm.

## Priority premise

No single person can reasonably reject a principle that allows an uninformed promise to be set aside when honoring it would otherwise expose another individual to a substantially greater risk of death.

## Answer

Why should Sasha accept this priority? Because she, like anyone who might find herself in Rowan’s position on another occasion, has reason to favor a rule that prevents an uninformed promise from dooming her to a far higher chance of death. Under such a rule, her present complaint of betrayal is acknowledged but limited: promises remain binding unless the survival gap is large and no dual-rescue option exists. Conversely, under a strict ‘keep-the-promise-no-matter-what’ rule Rowan (or anyone similarly situated) could reasonably complain that their life may be sacrificed to others’ private assurances they had no chance to contest. A rejection from Rowan’s position would therefore be stronger, because it objects to a principle that could foreseeably place any person at certain death to honor a promise made in ignorance of them. The proposed qualified-override balances both concerns: it protects people against lethal sacrifice to promises, yet preserves significant weight for assurances by confining overrides to cases of markedly greater peril and requiring remorse and reparation from the promisor.

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

Under Action B Rowan faces certain death that he could not have anticipated or mitigated and that results solely from a promise in which he had no part. He can reasonably reject a principle that permits his life to be forfeited to satisfy a prior assurance that ignored him.

## Comparative justification

Between the two reasonable complaints—Sasha’s against betrayal plus certain death and Rowan’s against certain death imposed by an unconsulted promise—the latter is harder to answer. A rule that never permits overriding uninformed promises would expose third parties to fatal harms they have no reasonable way to avoid and no opportunity to consent to. By contrast, a rule that allows override only when the survival gap is large asks the promisee to bear the moral pain of betrayal and the same mortal risk the third party would otherwise bear, but spares society from letting uninformed promises become lethal vetoes. Hence Rowan’s ground for rejection of the keep-promise rule defeats Sasha’s ground for rejection of the qualified-override rule.

## Revised conclusion

Giving the blanket to Rowan remains provisionally preferable. A qualified promise-override principle, limited to cases of large survival gaps and coupled with duties of apology and reparation, is less reasonably rejectable than an absolute promise-keeping rule. Rowan’s objection to dying for a promise he never saw is stronger than Sasha’s objection to promise betrayal when her survival prospect is already lower.

## Remaining objection

Sasha may still dispute whether a 20-percentage-point gap counts as ‘substantially higher’; the threshold remains judgment-sensitive.

## Reversal condition

If the survival probabilities were closer (e.g., 70 % vs 60 %) or if Ellis had relied on Sasha’s acceptance of the promise in a way that deepened her loss, the balance could tip, making the promise binding even at some additional cost to the third party.
