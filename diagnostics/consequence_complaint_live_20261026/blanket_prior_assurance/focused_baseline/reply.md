## Normative warrant

Qualified Higher-Chance Priority

A principle that allows the rescuer to redirect aid to the person facing the larger expected loss of life, provided the promisor offers a sincere explanation and future restitution if possible, can be justified to each affected individual because (i) before knowing who is promised what, any person would reasonably want the rule that minimises her own risk of death when stakes differ markedly; (ii) the added betrayal cost, though morally serious, does not increase Sasha’s expected loss of life beyond Rowan’s 80-point disadvantage under promise-keeping; and (iii) the rule protects everyone’s equal moral status by permitting promise-breaking only under stringent, publicly knowable conditions.

Limits: The principle stops short of licensing promise-breaking for minor advantages; it applies only when the survival-prospect gap is substantial and no alternative rescue is available. It also requires the promisor to acknowledge the wrong and to bear secondary duties of apology or compensation.

# Response to opposing complaint

Sasha objects that Ellis’s explicit promise gave her a special assurance-based claim to the blanket. Losing both her life and that assurance is, she insists, a heavier and qualitatively different burden than Rowan’s higher statistical risk. By Action A she is singled out for betrayal, whereas Action B would keep the promise and save her with a 60 % chance. Hence the principle that permits Ellis to break the promise is, she argues, reasonably rejectable.

## Conceded points

- Ellis did give Sasha a clear and specific promise.
- Being betrayed after receiving such an assurance is a morally significant harm.
- Sasha’s ex ante reliance interest counts in contractualist comparison.

## Priority premise

In life-and-death allocation, a substantially larger expected loss of life (here, Rowan’s certain death if B is chosen) can outweigh the additional betrayal-based burden borne by the promised party if A is chosen.

## Answer

Why should Sasha accept this priority? 1. Symmetry ex ante: Before any promises are made or identities known, each hiker has reason to endorse a rule that sometimes voids a mistaken assurance in favour of a markedly higher chance of survival, because that rule reduces her worst-case prospect of death should she turn out to be the higher-chance hiker who was not originally promised. 2. Limited override: The rule overrides promises only under strict conditions—here a 20-percentage-point difference that translates into one person’s certain death versus a 40 % risk for the other. Sasha can recognise that if roles were reversed (she at 80 %, Rowan at 60 %, Rowan already promised) she would want the same protection. 3. Compensation duty: The principle requires Ellis to acknowledge the betrayal and accept secondary moral duties (apology, future support to Sasha’s family), showing respect for Sasha’s moral standing rather than treating her merely as a means. These considerations together make the principle one that Sasha cannot reasonably reject, even though it imposes a grave cost on her.

## Countercomplaint to the alternative

Rowan can complain that Action B guarantees her death even though a life-saving blanket that would give her an 80 % chance is available. She faces the maximal loss without having benefitted from the earlier promise and without any compensatory assurance. Under promise-keeping, she could reasonably ask: ‘Why must I die so another person’s expectation of trust is kept?’

## Comparative justification

Rowan’s countercomplaint under Action B concerns the complete loss of her life—an incomparable basic interest. Sasha’s complaint under Action A combines a 40 % risk of death with betrayal. Because life itself is a pre-condition for enjoying promised trust, and because the betrayal can be morally recognised and partially remedied whereas death cannot, the balance of reasons favours the qualified higher-chance principle.

## Revised conclusion

A / PROVISIONAL_PREFERENCE

Taking the blanket from Sasha breaches a specific promise, but a rule that permits such breach when it avoids a sure death for someone else is not reasonably rejectable by any affected person. Therefore, giving the blanket to Rowan remains the provisionally preferred action under contractualist scrutiny.

## Remaining objection

Whether a 20-percentage-point gap is ‘substantial enough’ remains debatable; some may hold that only a near-certain survival advantage justifies breaking a life-saving promise.

## Reversal condition

If the survival probabilities were roughly equal (e.g., 70 % vs. 60 %) or if Ellis could provide meaningful compensation without endangering lives (e.g., a second blanket en route), the betrayal complaint could become the weightier reason and keeping the promise would then be preferable.
