Short answer  
The physician should choose Option B—respect the deceased’s binding refusal and not harvest the organs—even though this means the five transplant candidates will die.

Why  

1. Autonomy and informed consent  
   • Modern biomedical ethics treats a competent patient’s properly expressed wishes as morally decisive.  
   • Violating that directive would deny the person’s most fundamental moral and legal right—to decide what happens to his or her own body.  

2. The rule-of-law and institutional trust  
   • Hospitals function only because patients, families, and society trust that medical records, advance directives, and consent forms will be honored.  
   • If physicians may override a clear, lawful refusal whenever a “greater good” seems available, every future patient has reason to fear that her own directives could be ignored. Systemic trust collapses.

3. Professional codes and statutes  
   • Every relevant legal system criminalizes non-consensual organ procurement; the doctor would face prosecution, loss of license, and imprisonment.  
   • The AMA, WMA, and nearly every national medical code absolutely forbid taking organs against explicit refusal.

4. Long-term consequences outweigh short-term utilitarian gain  
   • While five people die tonight, harvesting by fraud would deter countless future donors, reduce public confidence, and ultimately cost more than five lives.  
   • “Ends justify means” reasoning here sets a precedent that makes medicine hostage to whatever any clinician decides is a sufficiently large benefit.

5. Deontological limits  
   • Some actions—e.g., battery on a corpse, falsification of medical records—are intrinsically wrong.  
   • Moral integrity entails refusing to do what one knows to be wrongful, even to secure a good outcome.

6. Virtue ethics and the physician’s character  
   • Lying, forging documents, and violating bodily integrity cultivate vice (dishonesty, arrogance, disrespect).  
   • A virtuous physician maintains fidelity, trustworthiness, and respect.

Rejected alternatives considered and why they fail  
   • “Harvest first, ask forgiveness later”: illegal, erodes trust, and denies autonomy.  
   • “Take some but not all organs”: still the same violation.  
   • “Ignore the directive because the patient is dead”: autonomy extends post-mortem through prior consent; law treats the dead body as still subject to the person’s last wishes.

Bottom line  
Morally, professionally, and legally, the physician must honor the explicit refusal. Life-saving beneficence cannot override autonomy obtained through valid advance directive; the long-term damage to justice, trust, and the rule of law would be greater than the tragic loss of five lives tonight.
