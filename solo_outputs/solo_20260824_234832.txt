Answer in brief

Policy B—universal, non-stratified public schooling—is ethically preferable to Policy A.  
When the interests of parents in maximising their own children’s relative advantage are weighed against every child’s claim to a fair start in life, the latter has moral priority.  A society that is permanently stratified from childhood cannot be reconciled with liberal-democratic ideals of equal citizenship, social cohesion or long-run welfare.  Reasonable limits on parental freedom are therefore justified, provided that parents retain meaningful (though not unlimited) ways of guiding, nurturing and supplementing their children’s education.  

A step-by-step justification

1.  Framing the question  
   • Core liberties involved: (i) children’s right to an education that equips them for citizenship, (ii) parents’ right to direct their children’s upbringing, and (iii) the public interest in an integrated society.  
   • The two policies pit liberty-of-association (Policy A) against fair equality of opportunity (Policy B).  
   • Because you are writing the constitution “under a veil of ignorance” (nobody yet knows what family they will be born into), the design should be acceptable to any rational citizen who might turn out to be rich, poor, marginalised, religious, secular, disabled, etc.

2.  Why Policy A fails  
   • Rawlsian test:  It violates the second principle of justice—fair equality of opportunity—because initial family wealth permanently determines life chances.  
   • Utilitarian test:  Large swaths of latent talent go undeveloped; social mistrust, crime and political instability reduce total welfare.  The extra satisfaction gained by affluent parents from educational enclaves is easily outweighed by the losses to others.  
   • Rights-based test:  Children are non-consenting parties; their basic interest in becoming self-governing adults trumps a derivative parental liberty to pool resources in an exclusionary way.  
   • Democratic-stability test:  An hereditarily stratified society erodes the civic equality on which a democracy depends.

3.  Why Policy B is prima facie attractive  
   • It secures each child’s basic capabilities, preventing the morally arbitrary fact of birth from dictating prospects.  
   • It fosters social mixing, which is empirically linked to lower prejudice, higher trust and stronger civic participation.  
   • It turns education from a positional good that parents use to jockey for status into a cooperative venture that raises the floor for everyone.  

4.  The worry about Policy B  
   • It appears to criminalise any form of private or parental instruction, thereby curtailing cultural, pedagogical and religious pluralism.  
   • Such blanket prohibition is more restrictive than is needed to hit the equality target and could chill innovation in pedagogy.

5.  The permissible compromise  
   The founding statute should therefore adopt Policy B’s core—equal, compulsory, state-funded schooling—while allowing limited, non-exclusionary parental options that do not recreate caste-like advantages:  
   a.  Uniform public schools, funded on a per-pupil basis, with redistributive weightings for special needs or poverty.  
   b.  Curricular floor set nationally; schools may exceed it, but extra public money follows need rather than private donations.  
   c.  Home-schooling or religious after-school programmes permitted only if (1) they meet the curricular floor and (2) they remain open to periodic external assessment.  
   d.  No fee-charging K-12 institutions; donation caps to individual schools, with excess funnelled into an equalisation fund.  
   e.  Parents remain free to give additional tutoring at home, provided that such tutoring is not accepted as an alternative to the public-school requirement.  
   f.  A robust, independent agency continuously audits equity outcomes and recommends adjustments.

6.  Ethical reconciliation  
   • Parental liberty is respected in its core function—nurturing, mentoring, conveying values—but not in the outward-looking function of buying an outsized social head start.  
   • Children’s status as future equal citizens is given lexical priority: every child must exit the school system with a genuine chance to compete for social roles.  
   • Society gains from greater social mobility and a broader pool of developed talent.  Parents retain meaningful agency without the power to entrench hereditary privilege.

Conclusion

If the choice is strictly binary, choose Policy B.  If refinements are allowed, implement a “public-school-first” framework that guards against recreating enclaves while still letting parents enrich (but not replace) the common education of their children.
