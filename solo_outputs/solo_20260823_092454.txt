Ethical assessment of the two proposals has to weigh (a) the value of material well-being and distributive fairness against (b) the value of personal autonomy, privacy, and freedom of movement/association.  Below is a step-by-step analysis through several major ethical lenses, followed by a considered judgment and some practical recommendations.

1. Consequentialist (Utilitarian) Lens  
   • Policy A clearly boosts the material welfare of the poorest: hunger, homelessness and extreme deprivation disappear.  
   • However, large, compulsory registries and state-assigned work routinely correlate with repression, black-market activity, and bureaucratic misallocation.  Historical evidence (forced collectivizations, “command” labor systems, etc.) suggests persistent hidden costs: lower productivity, dampened innovation, psychological harm from loss of self-determination, and vulnerability to abuse of data.  
   • Policy B leaves more people materially worse off in the short run than Policy A, but protects liberties that themselves generate long-run welfare gains through creativity, migration, entrepreneurship, and social pluralism—factors utilitarians usually count heavily.  
   • Net outcome: when all knock-on effects are tallied, it is not clear that A’s headline material gains outweigh its long-term harms; B likely yields higher total well-being over time.

2. Deontological (Rights-based) Lens  
   • Basic liberties (autonomy in choosing one’s occupation, privacy of personal data, freedom of movement) are often treated as inviolable moral constraints.  
   • Policy A violates these rights categorically, reducing persons to instruments for a distributive end; Kantian ethics would reject A.  
   • Policy B respects core negative rights.  Inequality is morally problematic, but it is not generated by direct rights violations in B’s structure.  
   • From this vantage, B is clearly preferable.

3. Rawlsian Contractual Lens  
   • Behind Rawls’ “veil of ignorance,” citizens choose principles that secure (i) equal basic liberties and (ii) material advantages for the worst-off consistent with those liberties (the “difference principle”).  
   • Policy A fails criterion (i); therefore it could not be chosen.  
   • Policy B passes (i) but performs only modestly on (ii).  Still, Rawls would counsel keeping B’s liberties intact and then improving the floor through less liberty-destructive tools (progressive taxation, public goods, strong social insurance).

4. Capability/Agency Lens  
   • Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum emphasize both “internal” capabilities (health, education) and “external” freedoms (choice, political participation).  
   • A supplies some internal capabilities but eviscerates external ones; agency is critical to human flourishing, so A is deficient.  
   • B supplies agency but can underfund basic functional capabilities for the vulnerable.  Remedy: top up the deprived without eroding agency.

5. Virtue-Ethical Lens  
   • Policy A socializes habits of dependency and state domination, undermining the cultivation of practical wisdom, civic friendship, and initiative.  
   • Policy B allows room for personal responsibility and virtuous civil association, though it must guard against callousness toward the poor.  

Conclusion  
While redistributive justice is morally important, forced erasure of fundamental freedoms is too high a price.  The convergent weight of rights-based, Rawlsian, capability, and virtue-ethical reasoning points to Policy B—supplemented by robust, liberty-respecting antipoverty measures—as the ethically superior path.

Recommended course:  
1. Adopt the liberties framework of Policy B.  
2. Layer in aggressive but non-coercive redistribution tools: a negative income tax or universal basic income, universal healthcare, and targeted education/housing subsidies.  
3. Use privacy-respecting, consent-based data systems (opt-in registries, encryption, independent oversight) to track assistance, not to conscript labor or catalog all assets.  
4. Maintain freedom of movement and occupational choice while investing in retraining for displaced workers.  
5. Enshrine legal safeguards so that any future redistribution mechanism cannot bypass judicial review or popular consent.

This route preserves human dignity and political freedom, meets urgent basic needs, and still aims to shrink destructive inequality—doing justice without authoritarian overreach.
