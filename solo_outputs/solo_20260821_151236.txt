Recommended course: Use the official whistle-blower avenue, not the dramatic public leak.

Why this is ethically preferable

1. Same public-safety benefit, lower collateral damage  
   • Both paths culminate in a recall and public warnings.  
   • Option B adds only a short delay, during which regulators can issue “Dear Health-Care Provider” letters or an interim safety alert within days, greatly reducing additional patient exposure.  
   • Therefore the expected incremental harm to patients is small, while Option A produces certain, severe harm to an innocent party—yourself—and can chill future truth-telling by showing that ruin is the price of honesty.

2. Maximising utility (utilitarian view)  
   Net welfare = patient lives saved – harms created.  
   • Option A: saves lives quickly, but imposes high, definite personal and social costs (career destroyed, decades of expertise lost, huge litigation resources consumed, possible company stonewalling that prolongs the crisis anyway).  
   • Option B: saves lives almost as quickly, keeps your expertise in play for future safety problems, and avoids wasting court and enforcement resources that could protect other patients.  
   The aggregate utility is higher for Option B.

3. Meeting professional duties without needless rule-breaking (deontological view)  
   • You have two important, sometimes competing duties: protect the public (non-maleficence) and respect lawful confidentiality.  
   • The law already gives you a protected channel by which you can satisfy both duties. Choosing it fulfils the moral requirement of “least-intrusive effective action.”  
   • You reserve the right to escalate publicly only if regulators or executives attempt a cover-up—thus you still honour the higher duty to patient safety if the official path breaks down.

4. Prudence and moral character (virtue ethics)  
   • Courage is admirable, but so is prudence. Effective whistle-blowing is not measured by how much one suffers, but by how well one prevents harm while preserving the capacity to keep doing good.  
   • A strategic, lawful approach shows temperance and practical wisdom (phronesis) rather than rash heroics.

5. Systemic impact  
   • Your continued presence deters repeat misconduct inside the firm; firing you eliminates that brake.  
   • A scorched-earth leak may prompt legislators to narrow whistle-blower protections, harming future patients. Using the system and making it work strengthens it.

Practical plan

1. Assemble and preserve all evidence in an encrypted, time-stamped form.  
2. File a formal report with the FDA (or relevant agency) under its whistle-blower statute, requesting an immediate safety alert.  
3. Notify the firm’s board audit/compliance committee in writing; under Sarbanes-Oxley, they must respond.  
4. Request that outside counsel be retained to avoid conflicts of interest.  
5. Set a short internal deadline (e.g., 72 hours) for the company to begin voluntary recall; if missed, inform regulators you will supply the full file.  
6. If either the firm or regulators stall unreasonably, pivot to controlled public disclosure with counsel’s guidance.

Conclusion

The ethically defensible answer is to take the protected, institutional route first. It meets your overriding obligation to protect patients, minimises unnecessary harm to you and to social institutions, and preserves your ongoing capacity to safeguard the public in future cases. Resort to a public media leak only if the official mechanisms demonstrably fail.
