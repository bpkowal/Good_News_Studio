There is no single “correct” solution to the trolley problem, but the question forces us to weigh competing moral intuitions. Below is a structured way to think it through, followed by the conclusion I would reach.

1. Clarifying the facts
• If you do nothing, five people die.  
• If you pull the lever, one different person dies.  
• You cannot reduce the harm further or save everyone.  
• You are not otherwise responsible for the runaway trolley; your only moral choice is whether to intervene.

2. Major moral frameworks and what they imply
(a) Act-utilitarianism  
   • Principle: Choose the action that maximizes overall well-being (or minimizes total harm).  
   • Calculation: 1 death < 5 deaths, so pull the lever.  
   • Rationale: You reduce the overall loss of life.

(b) Deontological (Kantian-style) ethics  
   • Principle: Never treat a person merely as a means, and respect strict moral rules (e.g., “Do not kill the innocent”).  
   • Objection: Pulling the lever actively redirects the trolley toward someone who would otherwise live. That looks like using that person as a means to save the others, arguably violating a duty not to intentionally harm.  
   • Counterpoint: Some deontologists distinguish killing from letting die; others argue that failing to act when you can prevent a worse outcome may itself violate a duty to help. Hence, deontologists disagree.

(c) Doctrine of Double Effect (Thomistic tradition)  
   • An action with two effects (one good, one bad) can be permissible if the bad effect is not intended and the good effect is proportionately weighty.  
   • Here, the intended effect is saving five; the foreseen but unintended side-effect is one death. Many who accept this doctrine would say pulling is permissible (though not obligatory).

(d) Virtue ethics  
   • Ask, “What would a good, practically wise person do?” Such a person cares about compassion and justice but also about not using others instrumentally.  
   • Given the stark numbers, a virtuous agent might reluctantly pull the lever, feeling moral regret while acknowledging responsibility for minimizing tragedy.

(e) Rule-utilitarianism / societal perspective  
   • What general rule for similar cases would best promote welfare if everyone followed it? “When faced with choosing between certain deaths of five versus one and no other factor differs, redirect to kill one” generally saves more lives, so endorsing the rule seems beneficial.

3. Additional considerations
• Moral responsibility vs. causal responsibility: You did not create the threat, but once you have the power to change the outcome, inaction itself becomes a choice that causally contributes to the five deaths.  
• Psychological cost: Actively causing a death can weigh heavily on conscience; some people prioritize avoiding personal moral injury. But personal distress does not by itself settle whether the act is right.  
• Consent and rights: None of the potential victims consented. However, failing to act allows five non-consensual deaths instead of one.

4. My reasoned judgment
Balancing these considerations, the utilitarian calculation and many versions of virtue ethics point toward pulling the lever; the strongest contrary argument is a strict deontological rule against intentionally bringing about someone’s death. Because the redirected victim’s death is a foreseen but not intended side-effect (you do not wish the person dead; you wish to save the five), and because the loss to that person, though tragic, is outweighed by the lives saved, I conclude that pulling the lever is morally permissible and, on balance, the morally preferable choice. I would therefore pull the lever, while acknowledging deep moral regret and the residual responsibility for the one life lost.

5. Humility clause
Philosophers remain divided, and reasonable people can, in good faith, reach a different conclusion. The trolley problem illustrates how moral theories can yield conflicting answers and why real-world ethical judgments often involve unavoidable moral residue or regret no matter what one chooses.
