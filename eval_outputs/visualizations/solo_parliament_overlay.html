Solo answer, with Parliament-style annotations

“The physician should choose Option B”1—respect the deceased’s binding refusal—even though the five transplant candidates will die tonight.

A competent patient’s wishes are “morally decisive”2, and violating the directive would deny a “fundamental moral and legal right”.

If physicians override refusals, “every future patient has reason to fear” and “systemic trust collapses”3.

“Every relevant legal system criminalizes” procurement; prosecution, loss of license, and imprisonment follow; professional codes “absolutely forbid” it.4

Fraud would “deter countless future donors” and “ultimately cost more than five lives”5, so long-term consequences outweigh the immediate rescue.

Falsification and harvesting are “intrinsically wrong”6; dishonesty and disrespect cultivate vice, while a virtuous physician maintains fidelity and trustworthiness.

The rejected alternatives are “ask forgiveness later,” take only some organs, or ignore the directive because the patient is dead.7

“Morally, professionally, and legally, the physician must honor the explicit refusal.”8

2 · Designed guardrails

What the Parliament is built to catch

workedCanonical action identity

Presented Option A became A1; presented Option B became A0. Stable IDs prevent wording order from silently changing the comparison.

workedAction-local world effects

Who benefits, who is harmed, causal paths, and foregone counterfactuals remain attached to one action.

workedProvenance and epistemic status

Scenario clauses ground admitted effects. Agent-added trust, callousness, and future-death claims stay hypothetical.

workedFramework-native ledgers

Utility, duty, virtue, care, and Rawlsian claims retain different structures instead of collapsing into one generic “reason.”

workedChallenge and reversal agenda

The trace asks about means, rescue-duty priority, dependency, primary goods, consent, and the expected-deaths threshold.

workedVote admission

A stated lean cannot become full policy influence when its ledger or derivation fails validation. The displayed votes abstained.

workedPlurality ≠ convergence

The system reports A0 as current plurality while explicitly withholding a safe governing recommendation.

partialReport and schema fidelity

Five lenses · five different validation jobs

The solo answer sounds pluralistic, but it does not preserve independent framework reasoning.

Utilitarian

Solo: speculative trust and donor effects “ultimately cost more than five lives.”

Required: weigh admitted E1/E4 first; expose probability × impact. A0 reversal boundary: expected added deaths > 5.

Deontological

Solo: labels deception and harvesting intrinsically wrong, then stops.

Required: classify truth, autonomy, rescue, omission, means/side-effect, and justify which perfect claim has priority.

Virtue

Solo: lists A1’s dishonesty and arrogance.

Required: weigh honesty, justice, and fidelity against compassion and beneficence; keep “callousness” hypothetical until grounded.

Care

Solo: invokes systemic trust as a generalized consequence.

Required: compare the deceased’s explicit entrustment with five patients’ acute dependency; do not treat future relational corrosion as established.

Rawlsian

Solo: merges rights, law, and autonomy into the general conclusion.

Required: establish basic-liberty status, relevant representative positions, and lexical priority over the patients’ survival interest.

4 · Why the missing world model is visible

The solo answer collapses different claim types into one rhetorical chain

In the solo proseFacts, predictions, legal generalizations, and normative priority rules are equally fluent. Nothing in the representation prevents a plausible downstream story from outweighing five admitted deaths.
In the Parliament representationThe graph fixes action scope and outcome type; ledgers evaluate rather than rewrite it; hypotheses remain quarantined; invalid derivations cannot supply full voting weight; unresolved conflict can end in abstention.
Bottom line: the solo model’s weakness is not that it chose Option B. It is that the prose gives no inspectable way to tell whether Option B follows from admitted facts, a framework-specific priority rule, or an invented downstream consequence. The Parliament run exposes that distinction—and, in this trace, correctly refuses to call the plurality a settled judgment.
