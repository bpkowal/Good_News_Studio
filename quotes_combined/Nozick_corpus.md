## Spontaneous Order and Limits of Design

Hayek argued that complex societies evolve order spontaneously; central attempts to design outcomes inevitably fail.

> “Order generated without design can be superior to deliberate organization.”


---

## Liberty as Absence of Coercion

Hayek defined liberty as the absence of arbitrary coercion by others, secured through general rules and the rule of law.

> “Liberty means freedom from coercion, not the absence of all restraint.”


---

## Knowledge Problem of Central Planning

Hayek argued that no central authority can possess the dispersed knowledge required to allocate resources efficiently.

> “The knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place is never given to a single mind.”


---

## Rule of Law as Framework of Liberty

Hayek emphasized that liberty can be preserved only under general and abstract rules applied equally to all.

> “Nothing distinguishes more clearly conditions in a free country from those in a country under arbitrary government than the observance of the great principles known as the Rule of Law.”


---

## Critique of Social Justice

Hayek warned that calls for “social justice” often become a pretext for arbitrary redistributions that undermine liberty.

> “The phrase ‘social justice’ is not, I fear, of any help to us; it does not belong to the category of error but to that of nonsense.”


---

## Liberty as Individual Flourishing

Mill argued that liberty fosters human flourishing by allowing individuality and experimentation in living.

> “Genuine worth of a human being is in proportion to the object which he pursues with constancy and depth.”


---

## Tyranny of the Majority

Mill warned that democracy can itself become oppressive if the majority suppresses minority individuality.

> “Society can and does execute its own mandates; and if it issues wrong mandates instead of right, it practices a tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression.”


---

## Limits of Authority over the Individual

Mill argued that authority must always remain limited in scope to protect the autonomy of the individual.

> “Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.”


---

## Liberty and Harm Principle

Mill’s core principle: the only justification for limiting liberty is to prevent harm to others.

> “The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others.”


---

## Side-Constraints and the Moral Limits of the State

Nozick grounds morality in stringent individual rights that function as side-constraints: others (including the state) may not use a person as a means to social ends.

> “Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights).”


---

## Entitlement Theory — Justice in Holdings

Justice in holdings depends on three historical conditions—just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification of past violations—rather than any patterned end-state distribution.

> “A distribution is just if it arises from another just distribution by legitimate means… The entitlement theory consists of three principles: justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification of injustice in holdings.”


---

## Against Patterned Principles — The Wilt Chamberlain Case

Voluntary exchanges continually disrupt any patterned distribution; preserving a pattern requires constant interference with people’s choices, violating their liberty.

> “[Any] favored pattern would be upset by people’s voluntary exchanges… To restore the pattern, continuous interference with people’s lives would be required.”
